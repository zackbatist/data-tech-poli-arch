Notions of Data

  • Descriptive
    The observable sets of characteristics, properties or features
  • Explanatory
    The evidential basis upon which inferred claims are either verified or refuted
  • Communicative ⭐️
    - Means of transmitting observations from one set of circumstances to another
    - Acknowledge the material circumstances under which data were collected, and presents them as outcomes of decisions and actions
  • So, before I go further, I’ll briefly discuss the notion of data and disentangle its multiple meanings.
  • It turns out, that when researchers say the word “data”, they mean a few different things.

  • One way they tend to think about data is as descriptive accounts of observed phenomenon.
  • So, consistent and precise records about objects or phenomena, used to document the state of a thing through a series of relevant properties.
  • In this context, data is going to follow a data model that delimits what’s relevant, and it will always paint an incomplete picture.

  • The second notion of data presents them as evidence that forms the basis for a claim.
  • In an analytical context, data are always fixed and stable, and precede the analysis.
  • In other words, data grounds interpretations in reality.

  • The third notion of data, which is arguably the most important in the context of this workshop, presents data as means of communicating observations from one set of circumstances to another.
  • So, the records made about objects of interest, which are inscribed upon some medium, and can be consulted, accessed or used as stand-ins for the objects that they have been constructed to represent
  • This perspective tends to acknowledge the material circumstances under which data were collected, and therefore presents the data as outcomes of decisions and actions, rather than reflections of stable knowledge.
  • Moreover, this third notion of data is especially useful for enabling us to think about data as an outcome of collective effort.
  • Therefore, data are cast as outcomes of social and collaborative interactions, and it is possible to investigate the “rules” that govern who may contribute to and extract from these commons, and in what ways.

  • To be clear, these are not mutually exclusive definitions.
  • Data are and can be all of these things at the same time, and people even refer all these aspects of data in the same breath.
  • Pulling these apart enables us to articulate what people value about data in different contexts.

  • This paper will basically accomplish this through a series of vignettes which demonstrate how technical and organizational structures work in tandem to structure the constitution of archaeological data.

  • However, I can tell you some key information about them.
  • Both are research projects based in southern European directed by foreign professors from North America.
  • They organized their teams following a common model, with a director who coordinates the project, various specialists whom the director recruited for their expertise in the interpretation of finds, a number of trench supervisors who lead excavation and coordinate data collection, and excavators who are usually less experienced students who operate under the guidance of their assigned trench supervisors.
  • Both projects relied extensively on advanced digital methods, but Case B was well-known as a digital innovator.

Finds Processing

  • So, as we’re all aware, archaeological projects uncover a lot of material, which must be sorted and processed to facilitate subsequent analysis.
  • In the cases I observed, finds processing began in the field, where objects were either kept aside or discarded into the spoil heap.
  • At both cases, fieldworkers divided these finds into broad-level categories corresponding to materials, such as ceramic, bone and shell, separating them into bags and buckets labelled with the excavation unit in which they were found, as they emerged from the trenches.
  • At the same time, they tossed stones that exhibit no anthropogenic qualities.
  • They also used sieves to separate loose sediment from small objects, collecting those that they deem culturally meaningful, and discarded the rest.

  • In my observations, it was almost always the job of trench supervisors to write on or otherwise dictate these tags and bags, to render the haphazard bucketing into a more concrete and lasting official record.
  • This is not an injustice, but rather a manifestation of their responsibility to ensure that the material is properly embedding within a broader site-wide system of knowledge production that trench assistants do not (yet) have access to.

  • Additionally, after hauling all the finds back to the dig house, excavators were responsible for cleaning and brushing the finds with water.

  • Throughout this activity, excavators talked about the day’s work and their personal and collective experiences, and speculated about the things they recovered and which they were then revisiting in a secondary context.

  • As non-experts, their relatively amateur analyses remain unexplored, but they still felt an impetus to examine these objects and try to develop insights from these experiences.

  • Cleaned materials were then left out in the sun to dry before being brought to a space dedicated for finds analysis and storage.

  • The materials were dumped on a broad table, and specialist’s assistants quickly sorted through them.

  • They discarded any non-anthropogenic materials collected in error, then counted and weighed all remaining artefacts, before performing further analysis based on their typological and technological characteristics

  • In these situations, supervisors were responsible for instructing assistants regarding how to process materials according to a series of rules.

  • Assistants were encouraged to act as mere sensing devices, as tools exhibiting an air of objectivity.

  • No aspect of finds processing involved maintaining a journal or recording substantial interpretations of the materials, which revealed a failure to recognize that any meaningful insights could arise during these stages of work.

  • It is also noteworthy that assistants were expected to classify finds on the basis of characteristics commonly recognized as more natural, while study of their anthropogenic qualities was typically reserved for finds analysis.

  • In effect, a distinction was made between this grunt work, which deals with materials, and specialized work, which deals with artefacts and ecofacts.

  • Finds processing was therefore positioned as a means to an end, that being the arrival of cleaned and sorted finds upon the specialists’ desks, where meaningful archaeological knowledge would be produced.

Sampling

  • Let’s take another example:
  • Here, Chris, Liz and Oliver are recording geospatial points using a DGPS, and each person contributes to the collective action in a unique way that corresponds with their position in the project.

  • Chris is an expert in operating a GIS and is the one who introduced the DGPS to the project.
  • He is a fieldworkers too, but this work is being done in service of the database that he primarily maintains.

  • Liz is the supervisor for that particular trench, and is more aware of what has been uncovered than any other member of the project.
  • She therefore coordinates with Chris to determine exactly where the points should be taken from.

  • And Oliver is a trench assistant who simply holds the DGPS receiver.
  • He is responsive to the others, and is instructed to hold the receiver as straight and as solidly as possible.
  • He operates as an extension of the data collection apparatus, in service of the GIS database.

  • We see here a cooperative interaction between Chris who drives the data collection, Liz who provides input based on her familiarity with the trench, and Oliver who is operationalized as a mechanism of the database apparatus.
  • Each has their role in ensuring balance between context-laden and seemingly-objective representations of the trench.

Finds Analysis

  • A similar delegation of labour is also apparent in the context of finds analysis.

  • I already mentioned how after cleaning the finds, they were dumped on a table and any false positives were discarded.
  • Following that procedure, I observed how Jolene, the lithics specialist, went through and sorted the remaining material, sometimes mumbling her reasoning aloud.
  • She physically grouped objects, following a general pattern for each excavation unit: cores at the top left corner, blades at the top right, and the extremely high volume of flakes occupying the main space in front of her.

  • After she settled on her arrangement, she dictated her description of the assemblage as a whole, in a fragmented tone that insinuates strong intention behind each word.
  • Meanwhile, her assistant, Maude, wrote down this speech.
  • Afterward, Maude recorded the quantities of artefacts in each category, as laid out by Jolene, and entered this information into a spreadsheet.
  • Sometimes, Jolene would create a new category on the fly, and Maude would push back by asking whether they should slot it under an existing label, and those conversations usually ran on for a while, addressing the broader history of these classifications.

  • Whereas Jolene was the source of creative knowledge production, Maude was responsible for embedding these insights within the project’s organizational apparatus, which often contradicted Jolene’s creative control.

  • Maude also maintained a checklist to track all aspects of work performed on the materials, including information about the materials’ provenance, who processed the materials, when the work occurred, and detailed parameters that structured processing tasks.
  • This checklist resembled the records maintained by the project’s database manager to trace what records await integration into the database and to ensure that all information was properly recorded.
  • These records existed to track progress and verify the integrity of work.

  • We see here another tension between the allure of concrete representations and disciplinary creative.
  • Note that the power dynamic is between database and domain knowledge, not between supervisor and assistant.

Informal Consultation

  • As the project moved forward, there was some trepidation regarding the confidence of these readings.
  • The site produced some incredible finds that challenged dominant narratives, though the sediments were extremely colluvial.
  • Additionally, even though an experienced analyst could recognize the actual character of the finds, they were very chemically weathered, which made it very difficult to photographically capture their features.
  • Sharing illustrations was also deemed inappropriate because of a perception that they were too interpretive and lacked objective legitimacy.

  • The problem they faced was that the media that archaeologists typically use to communicate their findings was unsuitable in this specific case.
  • To resolve this issue, they decided to call in a well respected third party to examine the material for herself, to show her the challenging contexts from which the materials were obtained, and to receive feedback on how to proceed.
  • In other words, they wanted to encourage a more direct engagement with the materials and reveal the unique circumstances that made the data the way that they are.

  • In effect, they relied on a consultant as a proxy to (a) get a sense of what others might think of their still-unpublished material, and (b) help communicate the extenuating circumstances that made the material difficult to present through conventional media.

  • And to be clear, none of this was improper.
  • It was all above-board, and the team made a significant effort to instill an expectation of independent review throughout the consultant’s visit.

  • What’s interesting about this is that in-house specialists are bound by the systems that enforce systematic modes of knowledge production.
  • After all, they must produce a spreadsheet, at the end of the day.

  • However, consultants are not bound by the project’s data management system.
  • They can drop in, look at the material, and leave without recording any of their observations, while retaining a deep understanding of the archaeological material.
  • In other words, they are able to grant some legitimacy to the project’s findings, without a mandate to scaffold their observations according to a formal data scheme.

Discussion

  • Throughout all of these examples, there were tensions between what the database expects, and recognition that the database is a limited window on the things it is intended to capture.
  • This is evident in the ways in which people were recruited to represent either the database or the things to be recorded by it.

  • For example, in the situation of finds processing, trench supervisors were responsible for ensuring that the materials were channelled into discrete buckets, in accordance with the broader pipeline in which excavation is situated.
  • However, when taking spatial points, these same trench supervisors represented the local context, defending the integrity of what they uncovered from an apparatus that does not really differentiate between local features without being guided by someone who knows the trench.

  • Turning back to finds analysis, the lithics specialist relied on her extensive training to systematically classify the materials, while her assistant served as the database’s enforcer.
  • For both of them, the database was an immutable entity that they were forced to conform to.
  • However, when the consultant was called in, they were free to make changes to the schema.
  • And it was then up to the primary lithics specialist to update the data model with those new considerations, and to re-formalize them into a new authoritative data structure.

  • Both accurate representations and clean data have great value, but these outcomes are achieved by hyper-focusing on specific outcomes and by increasing our awareness of our interventions, which are opposed forces.
  • So the challenge is: how do we get both valuable outcomes — accurate and clean representations — while mitigating against the means through which we achieve either of them.

  • I’ve always been amazed by the sense that “somehow, this works”.
  • Somehow, we are able to achieve some balanced perspective.

  • And what I came to realize is that framing this as a technical problem is a dead-end.
  • The ways people actually communicate, take on specific roles in relation to the data structure, as representatives of the here-and-now or of the projected outcomes, is what makes this work.
  • And we’re always made to embody each of these perspectives, in various situations.
  • As a director doing directorial tasks, you have to think about how the work will be represented to various stakeholders, but as soon as he enters the trench, he is thinking only “is this a bone, or a stone?”

  • And we all have that excavation experience, we’ve all seen how the sausage is made, we all know that things are not as clean as they appear on the page or the screen.
  • We carry that knowledge as we do analysis based on structured data, and we re-introduce context as we write up our findings.

  • This is not the case for data scientists, who tend to intervene with little understanding of how data came to be.
  • In other words, data scientists’ obtain power through their ability to take data at face value, and for that to be considered a valuable quality.

  • When we make our data openly accessible, we are handing off our ability to communicate the limits surrounding data, and to ensure that others listen.

  • When archaeologists do reuse data, it tends to occur in-house, by people who are already very involved in the data’s formation.
  • Or, when data reuse occurs in a distant environment, analysts tend to request additional information that is not in the official documentation.
  • Excavation journals, for instance, are extremely sought after in contexts of data reuse, if reuse is being led by an archaeologist.
  • However, if it is being led by a data scientist, they just want the schema and nothing else that may complicate their view.

  • Perhaps I’m admonishing data scientists a bit too much here, but this is just to reiterate the fact that they are not accounted for in the ways we organize our work.
  • We may have people who deal with data, but we call them archaeologists, they are part of our community of practice and have obtained an archaeological sensibility through fieldwork experience.

  • This sensibility enables us to understand when it’s possible to bend the rules, when we need to account for context, when we need to be more structured.
  • This understanding, which is embedded in our social and collaborative relations, rather than in any technical apparatus, is what makes it work, somehow!

  • So, I guess I’ll end by reiterating that all archaeological data are objects of anthropogenic origin, and carry traces of the decisions and actions that informed their creation.
  • Also, because data are products of creative work, they should be treated as entities that communicate meaning between contexts of creation and reuse.
  • You can’t force an artist to explain his painting for all gallery visitors until the end of time, and people should be free to obtain their own meanings from a work of art.
  • So as we move forward with developing open data platforms, we should be aware of these concerns about creativity and control over the long-run, and whether we should — or even can — accomplish effective data reuse without any social or collaborative component.
